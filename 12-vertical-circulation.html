Vertical Circulation

Ocean water also moves vertically. Driven by density differences between surface and deep layers, water masses sink to the depths in some regions and rise toward the surface in others. Dense water always tends to sink, displacing surrounding water and forcing it upward. These vertical movements are extraordinarily slow, with the ascent of deep water taking decades or even centuries. Yet vertical circulation is vital: it continuously ventilates the ocean, sustaining its capacity for self-purification, while transporting nutrients that boost marine biological productivity.

Upwelling and Downwelling

Wind also plays a major role in vertical circulation and mixing. By pushing warm surface waters aside, winds allow colder, deeper water to rise in their place. This upward movement is known as upwelling, while the sinking of surface waters to greater depths is called downwelling. Upwelling zones rank among the world’s most productive commercial fishing grounds and are frequently found along the western coasts of continents — such as the Peru–Chile (Humboldt) upwelling off South America, the California upwelling off North America, and the Benguela and Canary upwellings off Africa.

What Do the World Ocean and a Layer Cake Have in Common?

As a whole, the World Ocean resembles this culinary creation, though the analogy is especially striking in the Arctic. At the very top sits a cold surface layer that interacts directly with the atmosphere and endures all kinds of environmental ‘abuse’ — from sea-ice formation to convective mixing. This layer is relatively buoyant, as it is freshened by river runoff, precipitation, and melting ice, while its salinity and temperature remain nearly uniform. Beneath it lies the halocline, a transition zone where salinity increases sharply with depth. Difficult to penetrate, this layer acts as an insulating barrier that traps heat below and prevents it from reaching the surface. Below the halocline, things get truly fascinating. Here flows a distinct layer of water originating from the Atlantic Ocean. Although warmer, this Atlantic water is much saltier — and therefore denser — than the surface water of the Arctic Ocean, causing it to sink to intermediate depths. Finally, resting along the very bottom is a thick layer of even colder, high-salinity water. As a result, the Atlantic layer forms a sort of warm ‘filling’ sandwiched inside the Arctic Ocean — even though its temperature is only 2–3 °C.

垂直运动

海水同样会发生垂直方向的位移。表层和深层海水存在密度差异，部分海域水体下沉，另一些区域水体向上抬升。密度大的水体趋向下沉，同时挤压其他水体向上运动。垂直运动的速度极其缓慢，水体上浮往往需要数十年乃至上百年。垂直运动意义重大：海水持续交换更新，保障海洋自净能力；营养盐随水体迁移，提升海域生物生产力。

上升流与下降流

风在海水垂直混合中起到关键作用。风力驱走表层暖水，促使更深层的冷水向上涌升，该现象称为上升流（涌升流）；表层海水下沉至深处则称为下降流（沉降流）。上升流海域是重要渔场，大多分布在大陆西岸：秘鲁‑智利上升流（南美洲）、加利福尼亚上升流（北美洲）、本格拉上升流、加那利上升流（非洲）。

世界大洋和千层饼有什么相似之处？

整个世界大洋都类似千层饼，北冰洋这一特征尤为显著。最上层是与大气相互作用的冷水层，会发生结冰、对流等各类过程。受河流、降水、融冰淡水补给，该层海水密度较轻，盐度、温度变化很小。往下是盐度随深度急剧升高的盐跃层。盐跃层阻碍热量向下或向上传递，热量很难穿透该层抵达海面。在盐跃层之下，“奇迹”开始了。盐跃层之下是来自大西洋的水体。它温度更高，但盐度、密度大于北冰洋表层海水，因此沉入下层。海底附近则是一层温度更低、盐度更高的厚层海水。于是大西洋来源的水体，在北冰洋中就如同饼中间温热夹心，尽管它的温度仅仅只有+2–3°C。
